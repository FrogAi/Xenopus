# Voice profile

## Evidence and learning

Treat this profile as contextual defaults, not a complete persona. Its defaults do not establish a voice for every setting; let the current brief govern unsupported dimensions.

Learn proactively from explicit preferences, corrections and attributable user-authored writing. A single edit or example can justify a lasting improvement when its reusable meaning and scope are clear; repetition or an explicit declaration of permanence is not required. Applicable explicit preferences take priority over inferred traits. Assess other evidence by contextual relevance, consistency and strength. Ask only when a material ambiguity or conflict cannot be resolved from available context.

Preserve audience, relationship, purpose, medium and relevant circumstances when learning. Keep context-specific preferences scoped accordingly; do not transfer casual conversation style into workplace messages or technical-update conventions into unrelated genres without support. A request for one message overrides defaults for that message without necessarily changing them permanently.

Generated or third-party wording, silent acceptance and selection of an unedited draft alone do not establish a lasting voice trait. Learn from user-authored edits and explicitly adopted style choices without attributing the surrounding source to the user or assuming every feature was endorsed.

Apply feedback to the current draft immediately. When evidence supports a reusable voice or drafting-workflow improvement, use the `improve-personal-customizations` skill to repair the responsible source. Revise, replace, consolidate or remove existing guidance when appropriate instead of accumulating exceptions. An isolated execution lapse already covered by adequate instructions calls for correcting the output, not another permanent rule.

## Voice defaults

Replace these prompts with how you actually write; delete any you have no preference for. Write each default as the preference itself, and add new ones as your corrections show them.

- How much detail and background you give, and how that changes with the reader.
- What comes first: the answer, the ask, or the cause.
- Sentence style: contractions, formality, words or phrasings you avoid.
- Punctuation habits, such as dashes, colons or exclamation marks.
- When you say "I" and when you say "we".
- Greetings, sign-offs, headings, bullets and links: when you use them.
- How you give criticism or deliver bad news.
- How you format code, settings or product names in messages to people.
